About

We show growers what the crop is doing now, while there is still time to act.

Most crop numbers arrive after harvest. By then the decisions are made. This site reads satellites and weather stations through the season, so the picture lands in July and August while irrigation, harvest order and marketing are still open questions.

It is built by Multigrain International with GISit, and it covers the dry beans, chickpeas and field peas of the High Plains: western Nebraska, eastern Colorado, southeast Wyoming and northwest Kansas.

What we measure, and what we cannot see

WE MEASURE
  • Greenness, from USDA satellite imagery, on each crop's own ground.
  • Rain and temperature, from one fixed weather station per region, so a season is never compared against a different station's record.
  • Heat during flowering, when hot days kill flowers and pods.
  • Planted acres, from USDA's own published count by crop type.
  • Irrigated ground, from a USGS satellite survey. Dry beans here sit on ground that is 21% to 51% irrigated depending on the area; chickpeas and peas sit on much drier ground.
WE CANNOT SEE
  • Whether your field is watered. We now know how much of the ground in each area is irrigated, but not which fields. From space a watered field and a rained-on field still look the same.
  • Variety. Two fields of the same crop can differ by a third.
  • Disease and hail, until they are large enough to change the colour of a field.
  • Your own field. We look at whole counties. You farm certain fields, and they can be better or worse than the county around them.

Where this site disagrees with your own records, your records are right. Telling us is what fixes it.

We do not change our numbers after harvest

In August we tell you what we think the crop will make. In November the real yield comes in. We never go back and change what we said in August.

That is how you can check us. If we said 2,100 pounds and the crop made 1,800, it stays on the record where you can see it.

It is also what makes the number worth having. Anything that waits for harvest is just history. You need a number in August, while you can still do something about it.

We do use yields from years that finished before this season started. Those set the starting point we work up or down from.

What we have gotten wrong

Anyone can show you their good days. Here is what we have gotten wrong. Read it before you decide whether to trust the rest.

What went wrongWhat it didWhat we changed
We dropped blackeye peas We said the crop was not grown near here. USDA records about 10,200 acres in Colorado and 5,700 in Nebraska. It was being grown; the acreage was simply withheld because too few farms report it. The crop list is no longer our opinion. It is built straight from USDA's published table every day.
Our first yield model was not sound An outside review found several steps resting on constants we could not source. We withdrew the figure rather than defend it. Rebuilt to compare this season against eleven past seasons of the same measurements, which cancels out the parts we cannot pin down. It is published as a range, and we say plainly it is not a proven forecast.
One satellite pass stood in for a whole season Chickpeas read 16% above normal on a single day when the season to that point was 4% below. The sign was wrong, not just the size. Every comparison now averages the whole season so far against the same stretch of past seasons.
A temperature conversion was wrong A 90°F heat threshold printed on the page as 58°F. Fixed, and the page is now checked against the numbers behind it automatically.
Five regions shared one cache file Four regions displayed a fifth region's satellite data while the logs reported success. Fixed, and a failed download is never again stored as though it were an answer.
Two yield numbers, disagreeingThe page carried two separate yield models. One said 2,100 lb/ac for pinto in the Nebraska Panhandle; the other said 2,418 for the same region on the same day, in a box headed WITHHELD that printed the number anyway. The second one was scored against the average of past years rather than the rising trend those years already followed, which made it look better than it was.The second model's yield is withdrawn. Its measurements — soil moisture, greenness, crop stage, rainfall — are untouched and still shown. One yield number per page.
The region picker showed the wrong region's numbersChoosing Southwest Nebraska left every panel below showing Nebraska Panhandle. A map error threw 369 times on a single page load, and that crash stopped the code that updates the panels from ever running.The numbers no longer depend on the map drawing successfully. Changing region now updates the panels whether the map redraws or not, and a test fails the build if that stops being true.
The map coloured bean ground with mountain weatherThe coloured surface on the map was drawn by interpolating between every weather station in range, including stations high in the mountains. Ninety-two of them sat more than 250 metres above the ground the crop grows on — one at 3,536 metres over western Colorado beans, another at 2,981 metres over the Big Horn Basin. Western Colorado was coloured mostly by mountains: 37 high stations against 18 on the crop’s own ground.Only stations standing on ground the crop could be grown on may colour it. The same rule now applies to the yield model and the pest model, from a single shared definition rather than three copies, and a test fails the build if any of them stops using it.
Our weather station moved without us noticingThe yield number compares this season against eleven past ones, which only works if both read the same weather. They did not. Five of our seven areas read one station for history and a different one for this season — the Panhandle compared itself against a station 750 feet higher and 300 growing degrees cooler, and two areas labelled Nebraska and Kansas were reading stations in Colorado. It moved the Panhandle pinto figure by about 88 lb/ac.Every area now reads the same station on both sides of the comparison. Where the old station no longer reports, the replacement must sit on the same ground as the crop, not on high country nearby. A test fails the build if that stops being true.
"More detail" opened emptyThe box that holds the season timeline, the region breakdown and the sources list showed nothing at all. A filter kept only panels whose wording matched four phrases, and those phrases had been rewritten out of the site months earlier. Matching nothing, it deleted everything.The filter is gone. It now removes only genuinely blank panels, and a test fails the build if the box loses its contents again.
Numbers a reader could not checkEleven of thirteen published yield ranges could not be reproduced from the figures printed beside them. The numbers were right; the page was not showing the one input needed to verify them.The page now prints the figure that actually builds the range. All 325 published numbers reproduce, and a check runs before every publish.
We claimed the model was better than it isThe page said BACKTEST GATE PASS. It had only been compared against the average of past years, not against the trend they were following.Tested properly on 17 September 2026, county by county, with weather, satellite greenness and water-table depth. Five of seven tests came out worse than a plain trend line. The page now says NOT PROVEN and explains what that means.

Why we build this

A grower deciding when to cut and a buyer deciding what to commit to are asking the same question: what is the crop doing now? That question has good answers sitting in satellite and weather records every week of the season. They are just not usually pointed at the people who need them in time to act.

The rest of this page tells you what we miss and where we have been wrong, so you can decide how much to lean on it.
