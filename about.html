About

We show growers what the crop is doing now, while there is still time to act.

Most crop numbers arrive after harvest. By then the decisions are made. This site reads satellites and weather stations through the season, so the picture lands in July and August while irrigation, harvest order and marketing are still open questions.

It is built by Multigrain International with GISit, and it covers the dry beans, chickpeas and field peas of the High Plains: western Nebraska, eastern Colorado, southeast Wyoming and northwest Kansas.

What we measure, and what we cannot see

WE MEASURE
  • Greenness, from USDA satellite imagery, on each crop's own ground.
  • Rain and temperature, from one fixed weather station per region, so a season is never compared against a different station's record.
  • Heat during flowering, when hot days kill flowers and pods.
  • Planted acres, from USDA's own published count by crop type.
  • Irrigated ground, from a USGS satellite survey. Dry beans here sit on ground that is 21% to 51% irrigated depending on the area; chickpeas and peas sit on much drier ground.
WE CANNOT SEE
  • Whether your field is watered. We now know how much of the ground in each area is irrigated, but not which fields. From space a watered field and a rained-on field still look the same.
  • Variety. Two fields of the same crop can differ by a third.
  • Disease and hail, until they are large enough to change the colour of a field.
  • Your own field. We look at whole counties. You farm certain fields, and they can be better or worse than the county around them.

Where this site disagrees with your own records, your records are right. Telling us is what fixes it.

We do not change our numbers after harvest

In August we tell you what we think the crop will make. In November the real yield comes in. We never go back and change what we said in August.

That is how you can check us. If we said 2,100 pounds and the crop made 1,800, it stays on the record where you can see it.

It is also what makes the number worth having. Anything that waits for harvest is just history. You need a number in August, while you can still do something about it.

We do use yields from years that finished before this season started. Those set the starting point we work up or down from.

What we have gotten wrong

Anyone can show you their good days. Here is what we have gotten wrong. Read it before you decide whether to trust the rest.

What went wrongWhat it didWhat we changed
We dropped blackeye peas We said the crop was not grown near here. USDA records about 10,200 acres in Colorado and 5,700 in Nebraska. It was being grown; the acreage was simply withheld because too few farms report it. The crop list is no longer our opinion. It is built straight from USDA's published table every day.
Our first yield model was not sound An outside review found several steps resting on constants we could not source. We withdrew the figure rather than defend it. Rebuilt to compare this season against eleven past seasons of the same measurements, which cancels out the parts we cannot pin down. It is published as a range, and we say plainly it is not a proven forecast.
One satellite pass stood in for a whole season Chickpeas read 16% above normal on a single day when the season to that point was 4% below. The sign was wrong, not just the size. Every comparison now averages the whole season so far against the same stretch of past seasons.
A temperature conversion was wrong A 90°F heat threshold printed on the page as 58°F. Fixed, and the page is now checked against the numbers behind it automatically.
Five regions shared one cache file Four regions displayed a fifth region's satellite data while the logs reported success. Fixed, and a failed download is never again stored as though it were an answer.
Two yield numbers, disagreeingThe page carried two separate yield models. One said 2,100 lb/ac for pinto in the Nebraska Panhandle; the other said 2,418 for the same region on the same day, in a box headed WITHHELD that printed the number anyway. The second one was scored against the average of past years rather than the rising trend those years already followed, which made it look better than it was.The second model's yield is withdrawn. Its measurements — soil moisture, greenness, crop stage, rainfall — are untouched and still shown. One yield number per page.
The region picker showed the wrong region's numbersChoosing Southwest Nebraska left every panel below showing Nebraska Panhandle. A map error threw 369 times on a single page load, and that crash stopped the code that updates the panels from ever running.The numbers no longer depend on the map drawing successfully. Changing region now updates the panels whether the map redraws or not, and a test fails the build if that stops being true.
Our growth model had no water in itIt multiplied sunlight by greenness by a heat term and stopped there. On ground where the limiting factor is usually water, and in a season that followed the driest winter in 31 years, the one thing everybody was talking about was not in the arithmetic at all. It had been left out because the archive held sunshine and temperature and no rainfall, so past seasons could not be scored on water even if this one could.Rainfall is now measured for all seven areas, pooled across every gauge that has reported for the whole eleven-year record — 15 to 41 gauges each — and a root-zone water balance runs every day of every season. Then we tested whether it made the yield forecast better, against every harvest USDA has published here. It made it worse, in all seven crop-and-state combinations. So the water is measured and shown, and it is not allowed to move a yield number. We say why below.
A water term that sounded right and tested wrongScored against ten years of real harvests, leaving each year out of its own baseline, the model without water beat plain guesswork in 1 case out of 7. With water it beat guesswork in 0 out of 7, and its average error grew by three quarters. We then tested the obvious excuse — that these are irrigated fields and the model was treating them as rainfed — by assuming 85% of the ground was watered. Still worse. Assuming none of it was watered was far worse again. The damage scaled with how much water we let in, which is the signature of a term that is wrong rather than one that needs tuning.The water balance is published as an observation, like flowering heat days, and kept out of the yield. The likely reason is that rainfall minus evaporation cannot see a centre pivot: the dry years it punishes hardest are the years a grower simply ran more water. So we stopped inferring the soil and went to find it measured. Anyone can rerun the whole test: python3 scripts/backtest.py.
We were calculating soil moisture when it was already measuredWe built the water term by working the soil's state out from a rain gauge and an evaporation formula. We did not need to. NASA publishes root-zone soil moisture for any point on earth, daily, back to 1981, on the same free address this site has been calling for its sunlight since the first day. It was one extra word in the request. We had been reading one column of a page and not the next.Root-zone soil moisture and actual evapotranspiration are now collected for all seven areas, every day, back to 2015. We checked them against the real thing rather than trusting them: USDA has had sensors buried in the ground at Torrington, 15 km from Wyoming bean country, since 1997. The satellite figure and the buried sensor agree day by day across 3,801 days, and both put the summer of 2026 as the driest root zone in the eleven-year record. Tested as a yield term it still did not beat plain guesswork, so it is shown and not used in the yield. It is better than what it replaced and it is honest about what it is: a model of the soil checked against a probe, not a probe.
Our method page described a model we stopped runningThe page a reviewer opens to check our work described "GISit v1, a regularized equation trained on 92 published state-year pinto-yield outcomes". That model was withdrawn months ago. It was not wrong in a detail; it described a different system. Anyone who read it and then read the code would conclude the code was broken.The method page is no longer written by hand. It is generated from the live data files every time the model runs, and a build gate regenerates it and fails if a single character differs. It now carries every dataset, every equation, the constants we cannot source, the tests we failed, and the commands to rerun each step. A page kept up to date by remembering to update it is a page that is out of date.
We published a swing the record does not supportThe season index moved several times harder than reality. Tested against every USDA harvest here, with each year held out of its own baseline, publishing it raw was worse than simply assuming an average year in six of seven crop-and-state combinations. For Nebraska and Colorado dry beans the model's direction was actually backwards.Every published figure is now multiplied by how much of its swing survived that test. For dry beans in Nebraska and Colorado that is zero, so we publish a normal year and show the raw figure beside it. For peas it is 1.02 — their swing was right all along. The scaling is fitted on 2015–2025 and applied to 2026, which is not in the fit, and the whole table is on the method page.
We called an estimate "soil moisture" on the first line of the siteThe most prominent sentence on the front page read "Soil moisture drier than normal in 5 of 7 regions". Nothing here measures soil moisture. Growers measure a root zone with buried probes at several depths, tensiometers or Watermark sensors, a hand probe, and their own rain and irrigation records; we have none of those. The map beside it carried a careful disclaimer that nobody scrolls to.It now reads "estimated water stress", the published data carries the same label on every figure, and a build gate fails if any page or data file calls a remotely-derived number measured soil moisture. The estimate did not change. What we call it did.
One invented soil for seven regionsEvery water calculation used a single root-zone capacity of 120 mm everywhere, and that number was chosen by us, not read from anywhere. The real figures, from the USDA soil survey at the centre of all 1,969 bean cells, run from 61 mm on the Valent sands of southwest Nebraska to 118 mm on the Keith silt loams of northwest Kansas. Half the water, half the time to stress — and the model could not see it.Soil type, available water capacity, slope and drainage are now measured per bean cell and mapped. Slope is published but deliberately kept out of the arithmetic until we can source a runoff coefficient rather than invent one.
Our first answer on remote soil moisture was leaky, and we caught it before publishingWe asked whether the accuracy of satellite-derived soil water can be learned where probes exist and carried to ground that has none — the question that decides whether this method works in a country with no instruments. The first test said yes: a correction predicted from a place's climate and terrain beat the alternatives. It was wrong. The "similar" places voting on each correction were largely places nearby, so the test was measuring that neighbours resemble neighbours, which is worth nothing where there are no neighbours with probes.We reran it barring every station within 500 km of the one being predicted. The clever method lost to the simplest one — the raw remote figure with a single fixed scaling and no fitting anywhere. That is what we now carry to unmeasured ground, quoted with ±9.6 percentage points of water content. The verdict code was also fixed: it had compared the clever method only against rivals it expected to beat, and never against doing nothing clever at all. Scored against 181 buried-probe stations in 41 states. Rerun it: python3 scripts/remote_soil_transfer.py --min-km 500.
We told GAJ to create a NASA account he did not needWe said the SMAP satellite was locked behind a NASA Earthdata login and asked him to register for one. He created it, then asked whether the data might already be available through Esri. It was. USDA's Foreign Agricultural Service republishes NASA SMAP root-zone soil moisture openly — worldwide, daily, back to April 2015, no key and no login. We had asked him to do work to solve a problem that did not exist, and to handle a credential for no reason.SMAP is now collected through the open USDA service and no credential of his was ever used. Separately, our first attempt to pull it returned nothing at all and looked like it had worked: the service silently caps every request at twenty days, so every station came back too short to score and the output file was valid and empty. The cap is now written into the code with the story of how it was found.
Our satellite-versus-model comparison was rigged by accidentWe scored NASA SMAP against the model we already use. On the "no fitting at all" comparison SMAP came out 18% worse than useless — because we applied the same fixed conversion to both, and the two products do not share units. SMAP reports water content directly; the model reports a dimensionless wetness fraction. We were measuring our own wrong constant, not the satellite.Each product now converts in its own units — an exact unit change for SMAP, a stated assumption for the model. Fairly scored, SMAP is better at 125 of 181 stations, which is a real result, and the median gain is nine hundredths of a percentage point of water content, which is agronomically nothing. On the Wyoming bean ground this site actually covers, the model we already use is the better of the two. So we did not switch, and both are published so anyone can check that decision.
We could not see winter at allOur weather record started on 15 March, so the site had no view of October through March — the months that actually refill these aquifers before a crop goes in. We were publishing how low the water was without being able to say why. Measured properly, last winter brought the Nebraska Panhandle 1.5 inches, 44% of normal, the driest of the last 31 winters. Southwest Nebraska, northeast Colorado and southeast Wyoming were also at their driest in 31 years.Winter precipitation is now measured for every area against its own 30 previous winters, and it is shown beside the water table so the cause sits next to the effect. Without it, "the water is low" reads as the aquifer draining, when in the Panhandle the aquifer is steady across a century and one winter did this.
We buried the water data where nobody would find itWe gathered the water readings, wrote them up, and put them 19,574 pixels down a 25,717-pixel page — about three quarters of the way to the bottom, inside a box that starts closed. GAJ could not find it and said so. Building something and hiding it is the same as not building it.The water now sits in the top block, beside the crop headline, 339 pixels from the top of the page. A test fails the build if it moves back down.
Colorado was left out of this year’s water readingThe current-season water figure was built from Nebraska’s state database, which covers Nebraska only. Northeast Colorado came back with five wells and western Colorado with none, while both happily showed a long-run figure — so it looked like thin data when it was a gap in our code. Colorado publishes its own readings; we simply were not asking.Colorado’s own water agency is now read directly. Northeast Colorado is 3.3 feet below its normal this year, from 128 wells.
We published a stale acreage figureThe page said Montana grows 260,000 of 541,000 US chickpea acres. The 541,000 came from USDA’s June forecast of what farmers intended to plant. USDA’s own state pages had since revised it down.The sentence now compares Montana to Washington using two figures read straight off USDA’s pages, so it needs no arithmetic of ours and no national total at all.
We assumed the water was running outGrowers told us wells were failing in northeast Colorado, and that is true — one county there is about 50 feet lower than in 1965. We took that as the picture for the whole area. It is not. Measured across 974,724 readings from 24,018 Nebraska wells going back to 1930, the water under the Nebraska Panhandle has fallen about four feet in a century. The North Platte valley is fed by a river; the Colorado high plains are not.We now publish the measured rate for each area separately, including the areas where it is steady, and we say which wells it was measured from. Where a region has no long record of its own, we publish no rate rather than borrowing a neighbour’s.
One sentence answered for a thousand milesThe cutworm reading took the middle value across every weather station from Kansas to the Big Horn Basin and reported it as one answer. On 12 July the page said flight had not reached the point where you should start scouting — while northwest Kansas was at 97%, southwest Nebraska at 60% and northeast Colorado at 32%. A Kansas grower reading that would not have gone out to look.The reading now answers for the region you have selected, and says so by name. A test fails the build if it goes back to one number for everywhere.
The map coloured bean ground with mountain weatherThe coloured surface on the map was drawn by interpolating between every weather station in range, including stations high in the mountains. Ninety-two of them sat more than 250 metres above the ground the crop grows on — one at 3,536 metres over western Colorado beans, another at 2,981 metres over the Big Horn Basin. Western Colorado was coloured mostly by mountains: 37 high stations against 18 on the crop’s own ground.Only stations standing on ground the crop could be grown on may colour it. The same rule now applies to the yield model and the pest model, from a single shared definition rather than three copies, and a test fails the build if any of them stops using it.
Our weather station moved without us noticingThe yield number compares this season against eleven past ones, which only works if both read the same weather. They did not. Five of our seven areas read one station for history and a different one for this season — the Panhandle compared itself against a station 750 feet higher and 300 growing degrees cooler, and two areas labelled Nebraska and Kansas were reading stations in Colorado. It moved the Panhandle pinto figure by about 88 lb/ac.Every area now reads the same station on both sides of the comparison. Where the old station no longer reports, the replacement must sit on the same ground as the crop, not on high country nearby. A test fails the build if that stops being true.
"More detail" opened emptyThe box that holds the season timeline, the region breakdown and the sources list showed nothing at all. A filter kept only panels whose wording matched four phrases, and those phrases had been rewritten out of the site months earlier. Matching nothing, it deleted everything.The filter is gone. It now removes only genuinely blank panels, and a test fails the build if the box loses its contents again.
Numbers a reader could not checkEleven of thirteen published yield ranges could not be reproduced from the figures printed beside them. The numbers were right; the page was not showing the one input needed to verify them.The page now prints the figure that actually builds the range. All 325 published numbers reproduce, and a check runs before every publish.
We claimed the model was better than it isThe page said BACKTEST GATE PASS. It had only been compared against the average of past years, not against the trend they were following.Tested properly on 17 September 2026, county by county, with weather, satellite greenness and water-table depth. Five of seven tests came out worse than a plain trend line. The page now says NOT PROVEN and explains what that means.

We would rather publish this than be asked for it.

Why we build this

A grower deciding when to cut and a buyer deciding what to commit to are asking the same question: what is the crop doing now? That question has good answers sitting in satellite and weather records every week of the season. They are just not usually pointed at the people who need them in time to act.

The rest of this page tells you what we miss and where we have been wrong, so you can decide how much to lean on it.
